Sunenblick v. Harrell
Check Treatment
Relevance: 93.8% · 895 F. Supp. 616, 38 U.S.P.Q. 2d (BNA) 1716, 1995 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 11402, 1995 WL 476175 · 46 citing cases · Filed on: 1995-08-10, District Court, S.D. New York
The document is highly relevant as it provides a detailed judicial analysis of what constitutes probative 'actual confusion' versus non-probative instances like misdirected inquiries or retailer errors. Specifically, the court rejects anecdotal evidence of misdirected calls and store clerk errors as insufficient to prove confusion among 'ordinary prudent purchasers' unless they impact a purchasing decision. The key supporting paragraph states: 'In sum, there is no evidence in the record that during the entire period when the parties were concurrently selling recordings under an “Uptown” designation a customer ever made a purchasing decision under the mistaken impression that MCA or Andre Harrell was the source of Sunenblick’s product. Nor did any customer decline to purchase Sunenblick’s products under the erroneous belief that he was an infringer of MCA’s label.' This matters because it establishes a high bar for proving actual confusion, suggesting that mere misdirected inquiries (like those potentially cited in the user's case) may be insufficient without evidence of actual diverted sales or consumer purchasing decisions.

RVC Floor Decor, Ltd. v. Floor & Decor Holdings, Inc.
Check Treatment
Relevance: 90.5% · [No citation found] · 0 citing cases · Filed on: 2023-03-07, District Court, E.D. New York
The court opinion is highly relevant as it directly addresses the admissibility of 'misdirected' customer inquiries and clarifies that not all confusion evidence is actionable. Specifically, the court rules that evidence of individuals contacting the plaintiff looking for the defendant's products is irrelevant unless it can be shown that those individuals mistakenly purchased goods from the plaintiff. This supports the user's need to distinguish between mere inquiries and actual purchasing decisions in their confusion argument. Supporting paragraph: 'The Court rules that any evidence Plaintiff may have sought to introduce pertaining to individuals contacting Plaintiff’s store looking for either Defendant, or Defendant’s products, and who did not mistakenly purchase goods from Plaintiff believing that they were dealing with Defendant, should be excluded as irrelevant under Rules 401, 402, and 403... To the extent Plaintiff’s evidence does consist of purchasers or prospective purchasers of Plaintiff’s products who made purchasing decisions based upon the mistaken belief that Plaintiff’s products were produced by Defendant, the Court finds such evidence would be relevant to Plaintiff’s reverse confusion theory and, therefore, not precluded.'

Trouble v. Wet Seal, Inc.
Check Treatment
Relevance: 89.8% · 179 F. Supp. 2d 291, 69 U.S.P.Q. 2d (BNA) 1603, 2001 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 20846, 2001 WL 1606895 · 36 citing cases · Filed on: 2001-12-14, District Court, S.D. New York
The document is highly relevant as it establishes the evidentiary standards for proving 'actual confusion' through customer statements, which is central to the user's query about misdirected inquiries. Specifically, the court holds that while customer statements indicating confusion are admissible to show the customers' state of mind (and thus not hearsay), written 'confusion logs' created by employees specifically for litigation are inadmissible as business records due to lack of trustworthiness. This distinction is critical for the user to understand how to properly present their evidence of misdirected customer inquiries. Supporting paragraph: 'Here, Troublé seeks to introduce customer statements to show the customers’ state of mind — that they were confused—as opposed to the truth of what they said. ... Accordingly, there is no hearsay problem with the statements by Troublé’s customers indicating that they were confused.' (Paragraph b348-11). However, the court also notes that the recordings of these statements (the logs) were inadmissible because they were not kept in the ordinary course of business and lacked trustworthiness (Paragraphs b349-11, b350-4).
